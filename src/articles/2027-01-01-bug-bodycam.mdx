George Tran, a current trial lawyer and former assistant attorney with the US Department of Justice, wrote in his analysis of the footage that Tinney’s comment prior to arriving on the scene “strongly suggests that the stop was influenced by perceived race, religion, or political expression.”

Tran said that could violate students’ Fourth Amendment Rights against unreasonable search and seizure, which requires an officer to have reasonable suspicion to conduct a stop.

Later in the video, Tinney tells Field Operations Captain Joey Williams on the phone that the students are “probably loosely affiliated, one of them was wearing a keffiyah, the rest of them were wearing hoods and covid masks.” Pittman did not directly answer a question about what it means for an individual to be “affiliated.”

Ben Nissenbaum, a Bay Area civil rights lawyer, wrote in an email that while he saw political bias in Tinney’s actions, officers ”appear to have the people arrested caught dead-to-rights on the vandalism charge.” There remained enough suspicion for a valid stop, Nissenbaum said.

In the video, Tinney appears to confirm the flyers for which the students were charged are freshly pasted by touching them. After stopping the four students, he finds paste on the scene.

Logan Matthews, another officer on the scene, wrote in the police report that he later obtained a confession from one of the students after arresting them that they had put up the posters.

Tran and Jayme Walker, an Oakland lawyer with expertise in police misconduct, said they believed the grounds for making a stop were weak. Tran cited Tinney’s immediate pursuit of the individual in a keffiyah and that he does not appear to confirm whether the students with the bug had actually pasted the contested flyers on Sather Gate themselves prior to stopping them. Tran and Walker said the validity of the initial stop was troubled further by what they saw as bias towards the individual in a Keffiyah.

“The lack of reasonable suspicion to stop her specifically is not a technicality. It is the entire constitutional premise upon which the encounter must stand or fall,” Tran wrote. “If the stop itself is unlawful, then the escalation that followed is constitutionally infirm from the outset.”

On the scene, Tinney estimates that clean-up of the 28 posters that the students had allegedly posted would exceed $400, placing the crime within the limits of felony vandalism.

A cost report from facilities management shows that clean up cost campus $1,644.50.

“Per statute, if the value of the damage perpetrated exceeds $400, the law mandates a felony charge,” Pittman said in her letter to the IAB. “The idea that UCPD’s adherence to the
requirements of the law was in any way "disproportionate" is not well taken, but more importantly, is belied by the language of what the law requires.”

Legal experts, however, said that officers have broad discretion in what to charge an individual accused of vandalism in particular. In California, vandalism is a “wobbler,” a crime which may be pursued as either a felony or a misdemeanor. The experts said UCPD could have pursued a misdemeanor, a felony or no charges at all.

The Demonstrations and Student Action Committee of the Academic Senate, which has been looking into the case since November, came to the conclusion that pursuing felony charges was not necessary after speaking with campus legal counsel, according to chair Trond Petersen.

“You can guess I am quite upset,” Petersen said. “This is not a good way to spend my time for me or for the university. But it’s much worse. Think about it for the 3 students.”

In the video, Tinney asks the students if they want to go to Santa Rita before he appears to make the determination of the crime as a felony.

UCPD’s department policy manual states that individuals arrested for a felony should be booked in the county jail, according to local policy. Nissenbaum said that Tinney’s decision to pursue felonies and send the students to Santa Rita betrayed Tinney’s desire “to punish the content of their speech.”

“That’s what part of the goal of putting us through this was: to make us feel afraid, make other people who see what happened to us feel afraid,” said Inigo Macey, one of the arrested students who graduated in Spring 2025.

In Santa Rita, the students described a host of terrors. Macey said they were placed in solitary confinement for 12 hours because they are trans. Another individual said they spent two hours in solitary.

One student said they heard officers at the jail joking that it would be “a fun night” with so many female inmates coming in. Another said it was so cold they saw people putting toilet paper on their bodies to stay warm.

After their arrest, the students described lasting anxiety and discomfort on campus. One of them, who is set to graduate this semester, said they had concerns about obtaining their degree as the CSC case proceeded.

That student said their case stalled for a time after a Feb. 25 email following informal hearings on Feb. 4; however, the students received a proposed outcome letter from campus April 10, after the Daily Cal had reached out to campus for comment on the case’s progress.

Mogulof said campus could not comment on specific CSC cases because of student privacy laws. He added that the pursuit of discipline through CSC is a separate and unrelated process to criminal charges put through by UCPD.

In the resolution letter, CSC Interim Co-Director Michael Mann says the case had fallen behind due to the CSC’s requests for additional information from campus.

According to Mann, UCPD relayed a response from the officer who originally reported the activity to UCPD while heading off-duty in which he said he saw two students putting posters on Sather Gate and two others nearby.

Mann determined that the students had more than likely violated policy 102.04 in the Student Code of Conduct, which denotes “damage to any property of the University.” The students may contest the finding and request formal hearings, or accept it and the proposed punishment.

If the students accept, that would mean disciplinary probation until May 14, or until conferral of degree, and the completion of a reflective activity on the principles of community at Berkeley.

In an interview prior to the latest letter, one of the students said their initial trauma from the incident had dissipated and been replaced by frustration with the university.

“If anything, this night has honestly propelled me into a mindset of freedom and bravery that I was not in before,” that student said. “Honestly I do not think it scared me or anything or made me want to leave organizing spaces.”

However, in interviews and statements prior to the latest letter, those arrested that night said they were still living with its impacts after five months of turmoil.

Pittman did not directly respond to questions posed by the Daily Cal, but UCPD reaffirmed its commitment to fair, unbiased and professional policing.

The UCPD officers on the scene that night, including officer Tinney, did not respond to requests for comment.
