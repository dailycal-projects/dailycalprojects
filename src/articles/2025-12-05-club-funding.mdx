On a campus where some clubs operate on shoestring budgets and others jet off to Hawaii or have yacht parties in Bali, the flow of money through UC Berkeley’s student organizations is anything but equal or transparent.

Berkeley boasts more than 1400 registered student organizations, or <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#5d5e60ff", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "2px 4px", color: "white"}}>
RSOs</span>. From <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#8f679c", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>student publications</span> to <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#e3565f", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>academic associations</span> to <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#95c361", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "2px 4px", color: "white"}}>
cultural affinity</span> organizations, these groups form the backbone of campus life. However, each RSO has a different allocated budget with some earning their own money. Some organizations manage tens to hundreds of thousands at any given time.

How clubs acquire and spend this money is a question among many in the student body, though it is rarely looked at in detail.

## How campus funding works

The only source of RSO funding that is entirely public is from the ASUC, UC Berkeley’s student government. Organizations must fill out the Annual Budgeting and Spaces Application, or ABSA, application in the spring to receive funding for the next academic year. The only prerequisite is to be registered with Organization Advising and Student Involvement Services, or OASIS.

ABSA’s own funding comes from the ASUC student fee, which is $33.50 per undergraduate student per semester.

The ASUC Chief Financial Office is responsible for administering the ABSA process and overseeing the ASUC’s general financial wellbeing. The ASUC Financial Committee hosts an internal deliberation process to determine funding allocations for different organizations, explained Jonathan Ngai, this year’s Chief Financial Officer, or CFO.

These allocations are based on an RSO’s historical spending activity, demonstrated need, sponsorship category and application compliance. “As an RSO, we’re looking to see if you’re using the money the ASUC is giving you,” Ngai said. “Are you using the money in productive ways?”

Funding levels differ across different sponsorship categories of RSOs, as well as how many years an RSO has been established. Student Activity Groups, or SAGs, are capped at $500 for their first year and $650 their second and third years; Student-Initiated Service Groups, or SISGs, are capped at $650 their first year and $750 their second year; and <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#8f679c", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>publications</span> are allowed up to $900 for their first year and $1,100 their second year.

The total ABSA allocation for the 2026 fiscal year is $1,766,000, with $1,193,175 going to the ASUC’s external budget for RSO funding and the remaining $572,825 allocated for the ASUC’s internal budget. Internal funding includes stipends for appointed officials and funding for ASUC operations.

The Daily Californian organized the 413 RSOs receiving ABSA funding for the fiscal year 2026 into 10 categories.

## How RSOs generate their own funds

Most <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#fed23b", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>business- or tech-focused RSOs</span> receive very limited funding from the ASUC. Some RSOs receive none at all and are only ASUC-sponsored.

Consulting clubs, which fall under this category, instead typically fund themselves through projects with industry clients including Google, Apple, Netflix and Adobe.

## RSO spending oversight lacks teeth

After receiving a budget from either ABSA allocations or other sources, RSOs process reimbursements, expenditures and payments through CalLink, an online platform managed by OASIS. All RSOs that receive funding or sponsorship from the ASUC are only permitted to use funds through CalLink and are prohibited from creating their own bank accounts.

Ngai said CalLink accounts are “jointly managed across a variety of campus organizations,” but his office later clarified in an email that “ASUC Finance is only in charge of the CalLink finance part for ASUC, Graduate Assembly and their sponsored or funded groups.”

It is possible for industry clients to donate to the ASUC and ASUC-sponsored organizations. However, the CFO’s office stated this information is currently not publicly available.

While ABSA funds have strict restrictions on what they cannot be used for, such as food or alcoholic beverages and travel outside the Bay Area, the ASUC does not track miscellaneous funding. However, ASUC officials reserve the right to look at an RSO’s entire funding portfolio, and Ngai said the CFO is allowed to temporarily freeze RSO finance accounts if there is a mismanagement of funds.

The Daily Californian obtained documents from ABSA-sponsored RSOs that revealed reimbursed expenditures on alcohol, despite the fact that neither ASUC funds nor outside funds may be used to purchase alcohol for undergraduate organizations — even if the payee is of legal age.

The reimbursed alcohol was mainly listed on retreat budgets. The software consulting club <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#fed23b", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>Codebase</span> for example, listed alcohol on their retreat expenditures. Tech club <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#fed23b", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>DataStory</span> spent about $500 on alcohol on its spring 2023 retreat, and <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#fed23b", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>Berkeley Consulting</span>'s retreat “snack list” from last fall includes more alcoholic beverages than snacks.

The <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#95c361", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>Chinese Student Association</span> also had alcohol on multiple receipts submitted by members for reimbursement from the club.

None of the aforementioned RSOs responded to requests for comment.

Additionally, when we reached out to 32 <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#fed23b", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>consulting clubs</span> and 25 <span style={{ backgroundColor: "#95c361", borderRadius: "5px", padding: "1.5px 4px", color: "white"}}>cultural organizations</span>, all of them either did not respond or declined to publicly comment about their miscellaneous funding and expenses.

The ASUC’s public ABSA allocations data provides a surface-level look into the variation among allocations for different types of RSOs. However, there is a lack of transparency in terms of how RSOs spend these funds, in addition to details about external sources of funding for RSOs.
