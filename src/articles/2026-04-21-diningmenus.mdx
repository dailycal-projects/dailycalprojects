Encompassing four dining commons and four campus restaurants, UC Berkeley Dining serves a diverse group of students including those living in the residence halls who are required to have a meal plan, and those who live off campus who choose to purchase one.

We scraped the Berkeley Dining menus from February 10 to March 10, 2026 to investigate patterns in food offerings, allergen-friendly options and sustainability across locations.

## How each dining hall's menu differs

While campus restaurants have fixed menus, dining halls vary from day to day. They are planned six months in advance and follow a four-week menu cycle, according to Critical Communications Manager of Student Affairs Communications Lindsey Michels. Berkeley Dining’s culinary team leads menu development, while also incorporating input from groups like Residence Hall Assembly, Residential Life and other student groups and campus partners.

“Each dining commons has its own culinary identity, kitchen configuration, and service model. This allows Dining to offer a range of cuisines and dining experiences across campus rather than duplicating identical menus at every location,” Michels said. In some cases, the design and infrastructure of each dining hall informs what is served there; for example, Café 3 is the only dining hall with a Kosher-certified food station.

## Diversity in menu offerings

Michels stated that Berkeley Dining supports students with allergies and dietary restrictions through allergen friendly food stations at each dining hall. Menu items are also labeled with allergens in-person and online, and students can schedule additional one-on-one support with a registered dietitian.

One concern of students like freshman Coolsjes Singhvi is receiving enough variety to accommodate dietary restrictions. Singhvi follows a Jain vegetarian diet, which means he cannot consume meat or root vegetables such as onion, garlic and potatoes, and he cannot eat after sunset. However, he does not follow all of these restrictions strictly because many vegetarian dining hall dishes have garlic and onions, and there is always the risk of cross contamination.

“If they have onions in a dish, just having those on a side so it’s easier for people who do the same dietary restrictions as me [would be helpful],” Singhvi recommended.

The Daily Californian tracked the percentage of menu items at each dining hall that contain various allergens for the past month, across the various food stations.

Vegan offerings were high at most stations and dining halls. Gluten-free offerings were also high at most stations, other than dessert. Students looking for halal offerings could find the most options at Crossroad’s Allergen Friendly station.

## Campus restaurants offer alternate options

Aside from the dining halls, restaurants located in various parts of campus are open during weekdays and have a static menu for breakfast and lunch.

Monsoon Kitchens boasts the highest percentage of vegan options at 86%, with Local x Design second at 69%. Excluding Golden Bear Café, all restaurants have over 50% of gluten-free menu items, with Almare Gelato and Brown’s serving the highest percentages.

However, the restaurants are lacking in halal and kosher options. The only halal option offered across the restaurants is the Halal Rotisserie Tri Tip at Brown’s, and the only kosher option is the naan bread at Monsoon Kitchens — which is a side, not a meal.

Students like Singhvi still “prefer the [restaurants], mainly Ladle & Leaf and Brown’s” as he “think[s] they give you the most food for how much a meal type is worth, and they're very convenient.”

## Berkeley Dining’s sustainability practices

One of Berkeley Dining’ principles of service is being socially and environmentally responsible. They prioritize sustainability goals including “plant-forward cooking,” sourcing sustainable food and reducing both solid and food waste.

Michels stated that Berkeley Dining donates 50,000 pounds of food to organizations like the Basic Needs Center and local food banks each semester, which is equivalent to about 40,000 meals. Berkeley Dining uses an AI-powered food waste management program, Leanpath, to track and analyze food waste.

Furthermore, University of California’s 2024 policy on sustainable practices outlined a goal to obtain 25% plant-based food products by 2030. Michels stated that UC Berkeley has surpassed this goal and currently 37% of the ingredients that Berkeley Dining purchases are plant-based.

Berkeley Dining menus indicate each menu item’s unique carbon footprint. A green icon means a footprint between 0-0.29 kilograms of CO₂ is emitted in the process of preparing the menu item, yellow indicates between 0.3-1 kilograms of CO₂ and red indicates over 1 kilogram of CO₂. Berkeley Dining uses the World Resources Institute’s Cool Food calculator to estimate the carbon footprint of each menu item including greenhouse gas emissions from supply chains as well as portion size.

Excluding The Golden Bear Cafe and Ladle & Leaf, all dining menus and eateries serve over 50% of low carbon footprint items. Every item served at Monsoon Kitchens and Almare Gelato has a low carbon footprint.

Berkeley Dining additionally implements sustainability initiatives by partnering with organizations like Cal Zero Waste—a facilities service committed to reducing waste on campus. Sydney Woolley, a Staff Associate of Cal Zero Waste’s ReUSE Team, worked with Berkeley Dining as well as the Housing and Dining Sustainability team to pioneer the reusable containers now used by Monsoon Kitchens.

Cal Zero Waste partnered with a third-party reusable container distributor, Dispatch Goods, to produce a reusable container with washing capacity. Woolley explained that they chose to start implementation in Monsoon Kitchens because they only use one standardized container size, rather than other eateries like Ladle & Leaf where items like soups or salads require containers of various sizes.

At the end of the week, all reusable containers are sent back to Cal Zero Waste’s office, where staff send them to Dispatch Goods to be washed and returned for reuse.

Woolley hopes to expand this process to other eateries in the future, and appreciates that Berkeley Dining has been very receptive to these changes. “I understand that it can be a little much sometimes to have students constantly telling you you have to change your ways... But the workers have been super, super, super helpful.”

## Newly established Berkeley Dining Student Advisory Board encourages student input

Student input into the dining process is always encouraged, according to sophomore Steven Wang, member of the newly established Berkeley Dining Student Advisory Board.

The board consists of 15 students and is led by Berkeley Dining’s Executive Director, Christopher Henning, and Director of Administration, Katie Urbanowitch.

Wang—along with a few students and parents—reached out to Berkeley Dining in March last year to share feedback. They cited a few alleged instances of improperly cooked food causing sickness in students, as well as concern regarding the availability and quality of allergen-friendly options.

After communicating with Henning via email, Berkeley Dining met students and parents on Cal Day 2025 to discuss solutions. Urbanowitch reached out to Wang and other students involved in this initial dialogue with the board application last semester, and the board had its first meeting in February. During this meeting, students discussed initiatives like operational and service improvements, optimizing the geographic distribution of Berkeley Dining offerings across campus and even expanding Berkeley Dining into groceries.

“I think it goes to show the breadth and capacity of the student advisory board to come up with new ideas and continue to propagate the mission of bringing people together over food,” Wang said. “We’d love to hear your feedback.”
