The Nobel Laureates of UC Berkeley

subtitle

date

byline

In 1939, physicist Ernest Lawrence won Berkeley’s first Nobel prize for his invention of the cyclotron, a machine that accelerated charged particles and introduced a new way to study the atom. Nearly a century later, Berkeley’s Nobel laureates have investigated how cells transport proteins, developed theories for economic equilibrium, and theorized that the universe is expanding at an increasing rate. Their work spans decades of research across campus and at the Lawrence Berkeley National Lab.

As this year’s Nobel Prize announcements began, The Daily Californian examined the history of Berkeley’s laureates, tracing the recipients and their connections to the university. While the university primarily recognizes 63 Berkeley Nobel Laureates, over 100 Laureates have been associated with Berkeley as alumni, short-term faculty or researchers.

“Our list includes alumni, faculty, emeriti and long-term postdoctoral researchers whose education and careers are rooted here,” said Will Kane, Executive Director of News & Media Relations for UC Berkeley. “We expect to add many more Berkeley names in the years ahead.”

The university’s list includes Laureates holding a degree from Berkeley, holding a multi-year postdoc or fellowship at Berkeley, or if they are current faculty, emeriti, or faculty who stayed until retirement. Other affiliations, including shorter research appointments or visiting lecturers, generally fall outside this criteria.

These 63 laureates span nearly a century of Nobel Prize winners. Some spent much of their careers at Berkeley while others conducted research here before moving to other institutions.

Twenty-nine of Berkeley’s 63 laureates are faculty. Twenty-four were faculty members or professor emeriti when they received their prizes; five joined Berkeley after winning.

Thirty-five laureates are alumni, including former students and postdoctoral researchers. They account for more than half of the laureates represented.

Four of the 63 laureates are women: Carol Greider, Frances Arnold, Jennifer Doudna and Carolyn Bertozzi. Three received prizes in chemistry and one in physiology or medicine. Doudna is the only faculty member among the four; the other three are alumni.

Berkeley has received prizes across five fields. Physics and chemistry account for 21 recipients each, economics with 11, physiology or medicine with nine, and literature with one.

Sixteen laureates have worked at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. All of them received prizes in physics or chemistry, beginning with Ernest Lawrence's award for the cyclotron. Their discoveries range from finding new elements to genome editing.

Several laureates also contributed to the Manhattan Project, the United States' project during World War II to develop the first atomic bomb.

Berkeley physics professor J. Robert Oppenheimer directed the Manhattan project's Los Alamos Laboratory. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Physics four times, but he never won.

Czesław Miłosz is Berkeley’s only Nobel laureate in literature. He received the prize in 1980.

Twenty laureates received prizes in economics or physiology or medicine. Five of the 11 economics laureates won during the 2000s. Six of the nine physiology or medicine laureates received their prizes from 2000 onward.

The number of prize recipients rose from two in the 1930s to 13 between 2020 and 2025, though increases are not consistent across all decades.

The average age of prize recipients rose from about 40 in the 1930s to 67 in the 2020s.

Berkeley’s laureates were born in 14 countries. Forty-seven were born in the United States.

A connection to Berkeley did not always mean working here when the prize was awarded. Laureates’ affiliations at the time included universities and research institutions across the United States, Europe and Asia.

Twenty-four laureates were Berkeley faculty members or professor emeriti when they received their Nobel Prizes. Their awards span all five fields represented.

Berkeley’s 63 Nobel Laureates represent 7% of all Nobel Laureates in Chemistry, Physics, Medicine, Literature and Economics.

For Randy Schekman, who won the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, Berkeley’s research culture has been central to his career. He recalled the “special cozy feeling” he experienced when first visiting the university in 1975.

“We don't have the deep pockets of a place like Harvard, Yale, Princeton, or Stanford. So we have to be interdependent in a way that is much less common. And we are all keenly interested in applying our work,” Schekman said.

However, Sheckman is worried about the future of research amidst funding cuts and the political climate.

“We admit many fewer graduate students because faculty can't afford them. Over the next two years, people are going to be closing their labs,” he said.

Still, Schekman emphasized the importance of preserving curiosity-driven research. He pointed to Jennifer Doudna’s Nobel-Prize winning research on CRISPR, which was built on decades of previous discoveries.

"I think my concern going forward is that we don't lose sight of that investment in basic science," Schekman said.

As Nobel prizes are announced throughout the week, Berkeley will be preparing for additions to its list of Laureates.
