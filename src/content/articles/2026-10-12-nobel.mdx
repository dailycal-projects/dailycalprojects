<section className="nobel-opening">

In 1939, physicist Ernest Lawrence won UC Berkeley’s first Nobel Prize for his invention of the cyclotron, a machine that accelerated charged particles and introduced a new way to study the atom. Nearly a century later, UC Berkeley’s Nobel laureates have investigated how cells transport proteins, developed models for economic equilibrium and theorized that the universe is expanding at an increasing rate. Their work spans decades of research on campus and at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.

As this year’s Nobel Prize announcements [come to a close](https://www.nobelprize.org/all-nobel-prizes-2026/), The Daily Californian is looking back at the history of UC Berkeley’s laureates, tracing the recipients and their connections to campus. While campus primarily recognizes 63 UC Berkeley Nobel laureates, more than 100 laureates have been associated with UC Berkeley as alumni, short-term faculty or researchers.

“Our list includes alumni, faculty, emeriti and long-term postdoctoral researchers whose education and careers are rooted here,” said campus spokesperson Will Kane. “We expect to add many more Berkeley names in the years ahead.”

The campus’s list includes laureates who hold a degree from UC Berkeley, held a multi-year postdoc or fellowship at UC Berkeley or who are current faculty, emeriti or faculty that stayed until retirement. Other affiliations, including shorter research appointments or visiting lecturers, generally fall outside this criteria.

<section className="nobel-closing">

UC Berkeley’s 63 Nobel laureates represent 7% of all Nobel laureates in chemistry, physics, medicine, literature and economics.

For Randy Schekman, who won the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, UC Berkeley’s research culture has been central to his career. He recalled the “special cozy feeling” he experienced when first visiting the campus in 1975.

“We don’t have the deep pockets of a place like Harvard, Yale, Princeton or Stanford. So we have to be interdependent in a way that is much less common,” Schekman said. “And we are all keenly interested in applying our work.”

However, Shekman is worried about the future of research amid funding cuts and the current political climate.

“We admit many fewer graduate students because faculty can’t afford them. Over the next two years, people are going to be closing their labs,” he said.

Schekman still emphasized the importance of preserving curiosity-driven research. He pointed to Jennifer Doudna’s Nobel Prize-winning research on CRISPR, which was built on decades of previous discoveries.

“I think my concern going forward is that we don’t lose sight of that investment in basic science,” Schekman said.
